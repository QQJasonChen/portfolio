Case study · Dutch Exam Ace

Grading speech the model could not hear

An AI scoring feature was confidently reporting pronunciation errors it had no structural way to detect. Finding that changed the product copy, the prompt, the audio pipeline, the free tier and the error messages — in that order.

ProductDutch integration-exam platform
My roleSolo — product, backend, AI
Traffic7,000 visitors / 90 days

Why this score is not a nice-to-have

Dutch Exam Ace grades a candidate's spoken Dutch across seven dimensions drawn from the DUO A2 criteria. Candidates use that score to decide whether to book the real exam — which costs money, takes weeks to schedule, and sits on the critical path of a residency application.

The score is not a feature. It is a decision input. A generous score is worse than no score, because it sends someone into an exam they are not ready for.

What was wrong

1 · It scored a dimension that was structurally invisible
The scorer reported pronunciation errors. But the pipeline transcribes speech to text first, and transcription normalises spelling — by the time the model saw the answer, every word was spelled correctly no matter how it had been said. Pronunciation was unknowable, and the model was inventing plausible-sounding errors to fill the field.
2 · The UI implied an authority I do not have
The label read “official DUO seven-dimension scoring”. It is not official. It is my approximation of published criteria, and candidates were reading it as the real thing.
3 · The failing bands were improvised
The rubric defined what a 6 and an 8 looked like. It said nothing about 1–3 or 4–5. Every score in the range that actually changes a decision — the range where someone should not book yet — was the model filling a gap.
4 · The free tier quietly contradicted the promise
Grading quota was one per day. A mock exam is twelve questions. So a free user finished a full mock exam and got a score for question one. The landing page promised a free mock exam. Both statements were true, and together they were a lie.
5 · Feedback came back in a language the user cannot read
The prompt said “respond in Traditional Chinese”. Production returned whole paragraphs of Dutch. The entire audience is Chinese speakers who do not yet read Dutch — feedback you cannot read is not feedback.

What I changed, and why in that order

First: stop claiming what the model cannot know

Pronunciation and fluency are now labelled in the UI as AI inference rather than measurement. The prompt explicitly forbids claiming the official rubric and forbids inventing pronunciation errors. This came first because everything else is a correctness problem, and this one was a trust problem.

Then: make fluency real instead of deleting it

Fluency is measurable — just not from text. Transcription moved to word-level timestamps, and the service now computes speaking rate, pause count and longest pause directly, then passes those to the scorer as measured evidence.

The principle The dimension survived because it became true, not because it sounded good. The one that could not be made true got relabelled instead of quietly kept.

Then: define the bands that carry consequence

Added explicit anchors for 1–3 and 4–5. If a score is going to tell someone they are not ready, the rubric has to define what not-ready looks like.

Then: make the free tier match the sentence on the landing page

Quota moved to 15 for anonymous users and 30 once signed in — enough to complete the full mock exam the page had been promising all along.

Then: make a model change survivable

The Dutch-language feedback traced to a preview model with weak instruction-following. I moved the endpoint to a stable model and shipped it behind an automatic fallback to the previous one. The fallback was the point: a model swap must never be able to take grading down, and I would rather ship a change that cannot fail closed than hold it until I can reproduce the vendor’s environment locally. The Chinese-output rule was promoted to the highest-priority block with worked positive and negative examples, then applied across the four other grading endpoints that shared the flaw.

Then: make the rate limiter fail closed

When the limiter's backend errored, it previously let every request through — unbounded spend on a paid AI endpoint. It now returns 503 “system busy” on the speech-grading endpoints, and deliberately not “quota exhausted”, because a misleading error teaches users to distrust the accurate one.

What I would want a panel to take from this

The interesting decision was not which model to use. It was noticing that a field in my own output was structurally unknowable — and being willing to say so, in the UI of a product I want people to trust.

Everything downstream followed from that single call: measure what can be measured, label what cannot, define the bands where the answer changes someone's behaviour, and make sure no single model choice can take the feature down.

Still open, and written down as such

Known single-point risk Twelve AI features still run on one preview model. I fixed the one where being wrong carried the highest cost to a user, and recorded the rest as known risk rather than treating it as handled.
